22  Steering security investment

This chapter gives executives and the CSO a way to prioritize security investment with measures, evidence, responsible owners, review dates, and explicit residual-risk decisions.

Security question. How can leaders decide which AI security work receives staff, money, and time?

Organization setting. A hypothetical organization operates several AI uses with different data, permissions, suppliers, consequences, and evidence quality. Leaders cannot fund every proposed control at once.

Connection through the book. Chapters 14 through 21 produced assurance, monitoring, deployment, adoption, and responsibility evidence. This chapter turns that evidence into investment decisions. Chapter 23 tests the method across different operating settings.

22.1 Program ownership

A register full of risks and controls does not create a decision. Each asset, use case, exception, escalation, and review needs a person with authority to act. An accountable owner can accept or escalate a result. The same person can require more work before deciding. Risk acceptance records a decision to retain specified residual risk. Review frequency sets an interval or event rule for reassessment.

CSF 2.0 places accountability with organizational leadership. It calls for cybersecurity roles, responsibilities, and authorities to be established, understood, and enforced, with resources allocated in line with risk strategy.1 These outcomes do not select one organization chart.

The program record connects each owner to a decision and a review rule. A technical lead may own a test result, while the Chief Security Officer may own escalation and an executive may have authority to accept residual risk. The record states the boundary instead of treating “ownership” as a vague label. These assignments give each coverage measure a reader and a purpose.

Program control. The governance workflow checks each active system, exception, escalation, and risk decision for an authorized role and a current review rule. It blocks expansion or risk acceptance when either is missing. Coverage is complete assignments divided by all active decision records. Maintaining the register and resolving authority conflicts consume management time. Informal approval or an incomplete inventory can bypass the workflow. Recovery pauses the affected decision and escalates it to the correct authority. A complete register does not show that the selected control is effective.

22.2 Coverage and exposure measures

A count such as “twenty systems reviewed” has no clear meaning without the total population. A coverage measure combines a numerator with the full population that the count is meant to represent. Examples include reviewed high-impact systems divided by all known high-impact systems, or tested sensitive connectors divided by all sensitive connectors in the inventory.

NIST distinguishes implementation measures, which show progress in putting controls in place, from effectiveness, efficiency, and impact measures.2 It also states that the source, collection method, and validation process for measurement data need definition so results can be repeated and compared over time.3 Repeatable data does not make an irrelevant measure useful, and high implementation coverage does not establish control effectiveness.

The mechanism starts with an inventory and a clear denominator. An overdue exception is an approved deviation whose review or expiry date has passed. The measure records missing items, exclusions, stale records, and overdue exceptions instead of silently dropping them. A hypothetical result of 90 tested connectors out of 100 known connectors shows 90 percent coverage. It says nothing yet about the ten untested connectors or the quality of the tests. Outcome measures address that different question.

Coverage decision. The reporting service checks connector inventory status, test date, result, exception, and expiry. The responsible role can continue, restrict, or pause use based on a stated threshold. The denominator includes every known connector in scope, including failed, untested, and excepted items. Collection and follow-up add operating cost. Unknown connectors and stale inventories can inflate the rate, while 90 percent coverage still says nothing about the amount of risk reduction. Recovery adds missing items and tests the unresolved population before the decision is reissued.

22.3 Outcome measures

Completion counts describe work performed. An outcome measure observes the result a control is meant to change. A rate divides an event count by a relevant exposure or opportunity count. Recovery time is elapsed time from a specified disruption point to a specified restored state.

NIST describes effectiveness measures as evidence of how well controls meet desired outcomes, efficiency measures as the speed of useful feedback and correction, and impact measures as effects on mission, cost, value, penalties, or trust.4 It also states that one measure may be insufficient for a risk decision and that resource limits prevent measuring everything.5 These categories do not supply AI-specific measures or establish that a control caused an observed change.

Every result reports its denominator. A harmful-action count has a different meaning across one hundred authorized calls than across one million. Prevented attempts, duplicate events, incomplete reports, and changed exposure can also alter the rate. The packet lacks a validated worked denominator for these cases, so the examples require testing before operational use. The measured outcomes then inform renewed assessment and change decisions.

Outcome control. The measurement pipeline joins action, denial, incident, recovery, and exposure records for the same period and configuration. It reports harmful completed actions over all eligible action attempts, plus missing or duplicate records. A threshold can trigger restriction or renewed testing. Instrumentation and investigation add cost, and logging gaps can hide failures. Recovery repairs the data path and recomputes the measure. Earlier decisions remain uncertain until review. A changed rate does not by itself prove that one control caused the change.

22.4 Assurance and change decisions

An approval is tied to the system and evidence that existed at the time. A material change is large enough to invalidate an assumption, test, approval, or duty. A renewed assessment repeats review after a trigger. A release condition is an evidence threshold required before deployment or expansion.

CSF 2.0 calls for improvements to be identified from evaluations, security tests and exercises, operational work, and incident planning.6 NIST’s generative AI profile recommends renewed model risk review after fine-tuning or adaptation to a new domain.7 These sources identify triggers but do not set a universal release threshold or prove that a proposed improvement works.

The decision record links each trigger to stale evidence and the required renewed assessment. It also identifies the authority that can pause or expand use. A minor interface change may need a focused regression test. A new action permission may reopen the threat model and authorization tests. It may also require a new incident plan and legal review. These decisions create evidence-based options for investment prioritization.

Change gate. The release service checks the component version, permission change, supplier notice, affected assumptions, required tests, and decision authority. It either permits the change or keeps the prior version. Permission can carry limits. The measure is reviewed material changes divided by all detected material changes, with failed tests retained in the count. Review and rollback capacity increase ongoing cost. Silent supplier changes and untracked configuration can bypass the gate. Recovery restores the accepted version and repeats affected tests. Passing those tests supports only the changed paths and cases they cover.

22.5 Investment choices

Exposure alone gives an incomplete priority. A priority decision uses business value, possible harm, and uncertainty. Control cost, legal duty, and displaced work also affect it. Expected loss is a probability-weighted estimate of adverse impact, but rare AI events may leave both probability and impact highly uncertain. Opportunity cost is the value of work displaced by the selected investment.

NIST says measurement priorities can consider risk strategy, mission, data availability, asset value, control cost, partial controls, risk assessments, policy, and legal requirements.8 It also says likelihood and impact need to be considered together because either alone is insufficient for deciding the importance of a potential measure.9 The guidance does not provide a universal scoring formula or validate probability estimates for rare events.

The decision can use ranges and scenarios when point estimates would imply false precision. A control required by law may remain mandatory even when its expected-loss estimate is small. A cheap control with broad benefit may receive priority over a costly control aimed at a narrow, uncertain scenario. The selected priorities and their uncertainty need a durable executive record.

Investment comparison. The hypothetical organization has 180,000 currency units and two optional projects after funding its required controls. Project A limits connector permissions for six assistants. It costs 110,000 and addresses a tested path that reached restricted records in 4 of 200 adversarial cases. Project B adds a model-behavior filter to one public assistant. It costs 150,000 and addresses a plausible misuse scenario for which no local rate is yet available. These exercise values support only the comparison and do not measure control effectiveness.

The intermediate decision record compares affected systems, observed evidence, possible harm, implementation cost, displaced work, and uncertainty. Project A has direct local evidence and broader system coverage. Project B may address serious harm, but the organization first needs a focused test that measures the path and the filter under its actual application conditions. The current conclusion funds Project A and reserves 70,000 for that test and other unresolved work. A legal duty or stronger evidence could change the order.

Investment control. The portfolio review checks each option for affected systems, required duty, evidence quality, cost range, displaced work, bypass risk, and recovery benefit. It funds, defers, or rejects the option and records the condition that reopens it. Coverage is funded required controls over all required controls, while optional work retains its uncertainty range. Analysis and delayed projects have real cost. Optimistic estimates or missing systems can distort the order, and the ranking does not establish that the funded control will achieve its expected effect. Recovery reserves capacity for newly observed failures and repeats the comparison when a condition changes.

22.6 Executive decision records

A funding, expansion, pause, or acceptance decision needs enough context to be reviewed after staff, suppliers, or evidence change. Residual risk is the risk remaining after the selected measures. A decision condition is an event or threshold that changes an approval. A due date sets the date by which an action or review is expected.

NIST measurement guidance says a report can state the indicated risk and its fit with risk appetite. The report can also identify the needed decision and compliance concerns. It can record audit effects separately.10 CSF 2.0 calls for oversight results to adjust strategy and for risk responses to be selected, prioritized, tracked, and communicated.11 Legal and organizational authority determine who may accept a specific risk.

A decision condition makes approval contingent on an event or threshold. Expansion may depend on completing permission tests and resolving two critical supplier gaps. A due date keeps a temporary exception from becoming permanent through neglect. Teams can reuse the record and its reasoning in the sector cases in Chapter 23 and the whole-system review in Chapter 25.

Executive record trace. The decision record for Project A identifies the Chief Security Officer as the decision role, the six affected assistants, the 4 of 200 test result, the 110,000 exercise cost, and the remaining risk from untested connectors. It approves implementation for the six systems and requires a repeated authorization test before expansion. The application team is responsible for delivery by 30 November. A new action connector or a failed regression test pauses expansion and returns the decision for review. The observed output is a conditional funding decision that another reviewer can reconstruct from its inputs and limits.

Decision control. Before approval, the executive decision workflow checks the responsible authority, evidence date, scope, cost, due date, residual risk, and stop condition before recording approval. It rejects a record that lacks any required field. Quality is reconstructable decisions divided by all consequential decisions reviewed in the period. Preparation and challenge sessions consume leadership and technical time. Decisions made outside the record or based on stale evidence can bypass the check. Recovery suspends the affected approval and returns it to the stated role. Reconstructability does not prove that the judgment was optimal.

Note

Chapter checkpoint. The organization can fund Project B only by cancelling the test reserved in the Project A decision. Should it do so when Project B has higher possible harm but no local exposure or control-effectiveness result?

Conclusion. The present record does not support cancelling the test. Possible harm matters, but the missing exposure and effectiveness evidence prevents a comparable investment decision. The organization should keep required controls funded and complete the focused test. It should also record the condition that would move Project B ahead. This answer applies to the hypothetical evidence and budget, not to every high-impact risk.
