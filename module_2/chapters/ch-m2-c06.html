6  Sigmoid and softmax

The three-example classifier produced scores \([0.9,-0.2,0.6]\). Their signs support a binary decision, but a score of 0.9 does not mean a probability of 90 percent. We need a probability rule that gives the scores a consistent interpretation.

Sigmoid handles one binary outcome. Softmax compares several competing outcomes. Both rely on exponentials, whose mathematical properties also explain how to calculate them safely.

6.1 Binary probabilities

For a binary task, let \(p\) be the model’s probability of the positive outcome. The other outcome has probability \(1-p\). The odds compare those two probabilities:

\[ \mathrm{odds}=\frac{p}{1-p} \tag{6.1}\]

For \(p=0.8\), the odds are \(0.8/0.2=4\). The event has four times the probability of its complement. For \(p=0.5\), the odds are 1. Odds are ratios, so they are not restricted to the interval from 0 to 1.

The log-odds, or logit link, take the natural logarithm of that ratio:

\[ \operatorname{logit}(p)=\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) \tag{6.2}\]

This expression is defined for \(0<p<1\). Probability 0.5 gives log-odds 0. Larger probabilities give positive log-odds, and smaller probabilities give negative values. A Bernoulli distribution describes one binary outcome with probabilities \(p\) and \(1-p\).

Logistic regression models the log-odds as the affine score \(z=w^\top x+b\). To obtain the probability from the score, solve \(z=\log(p/(1-p))\). Exponentiating gives \(e^z=p/(1-p)\). Multiplying by \(1-p\) and collecting terms gives \(p=e^z/(1+e^z)\). Dividing numerator and denominator by \(e^z\) yields the sigmoid function:

\[ \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-z)} \tag{6.3}\]

The symbol \(\exp(a)\) means \(e^a\), where \(e\approx2.718\). For finite real \(z\), sigmoid returns a value strictly between 0 and 1. At \(z=0\), it returns \(1/(1+1)=0.5\). Positive scores favor the positive outcome, while negative scores favor its complement.

For \(z=2\), the probability is \(1/(1+e^{-2})\approx0.8808\). Using the unrounded probability gives odds \(e^2\approx7.389\) and log-odds 2. Rounding \(p\) to 0.881 before dividing gives odds about 7.40, so small discrepancies in displayed values come from rounding. Adding one unit to a score multiplies its odds by \(e\).

Return to the three-example batch from Chapter 5. Applying sigmoid separately gives:

  • \(z_1=0.9\): \(p_1=1/(1+e^{-0.9})\approx0.711\).
  • \(z_2=-0.2\): \(p_2=1/(1+e^{0.2})\approx0.450\).
  • \(z_3=0.6\): \(p_3=1/(1+e^{-0.6})\approx0.646\).

These are probabilities for three different examples. They need not sum to 1. Each has its own complementary probability, such as \(1-0.711=0.289\) for the first example’s negative outcome.

The sigmoid curve flattens near its extremes. A fixed change in a very large score causes only a small change in the displayed probability. That describes the probability function’s local sensitivity. It does not by itself establish that a confidently wrong model learns slowly. The gradient derivation includes the loss function when answering that question.

A binary probability needs only one score because the other probability is its complement. Several mutually exclusive classes require a joint normalization across their competing scores.

6.2 Competing outcomes

Suppose one document can receive exactly one of three topics. Its model produces a logit vector, one score per topic. A categorical distribution assigns a probability to each of these mutually exclusive outcomes. All probabilities must be nonnegative and sum to 1.

Softmax obtains that distribution by exponentiating the scores and dividing by their total:

\[ p_i=\frac{\exp(z_i)}{\sum_j\exp(z_j)} \tag{6.4}\]

Here \(z_i\) is the score for candidate \(i\), and \(p_i\) is its probability. The sum includes all candidates for this one example. Exponentiation makes finite scores positive. Division by their sum normalizes the vector, so adding the resulting probabilities returns 1.

For scores \(z=(2,1,0)\), the exponentials are approximately \((7.389,2.718,1)\). Their sum is 11.107, giving probabilities \((0.665,0.245,0.090)\). The highest score remains the most likely candidate, but every probability depends on the competitors as well.

The probability ratio for two candidates is \(p_i/p_j=\exp(z_i-z_j)\) because the common denominator cancels. Only score differences matter. Adding the same constant to every score preserves those differences and the full distribution. Increasing just one score increases its probability and decreases the probabilities assigned to the other candidates.

The axis of normalization is therefore part of the model’s meaning. For scores of shape \([B,K]\), each of the \(B\) examples needs its own distribution over \(K\) classes. Normalizing the last axis produces that result. Normalizing the batch axis would make unrelated documents compete with each other.

This also distinguishes multiclass from multilabel prediction. If several labels may apply together, independent sigmoid outputs can describe their individual probabilities. They are not required to sum to 1. A softmax distribution instead encodes a choice of one candidate among the supplied alternatives.

The following runnable PyTorch example preserves both cases. It applies sigmoid to the three binary logits and softmax to two rows of three class logits. The second class row contains equal scores, so its probabilities are all one third.

Code example: Sigmoid for binary logits and softmax for multiclass logits

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

binary_logits = torch.tensor([0.9, -0.2, 0.6])
binary_probabilities = torch.sigmoid(binary_logits)

class_logits = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0, 0.0], [0.2, 0.2, 0.2]])
class_probabilities = F.softmax(class_logits, dim=-1)

print(binary_probabilities)
print(class_probabilities)
print(class_probabilities.sum(dim=-1))

The binary output has shape \([3]\). The categorical output has shape \([2,3]\), and each row sums to 1. A normalized model output is not automatically a reliable estimate of real-world frequencies. Evaluation will compare confidence with observed correctness.

A categorical distribution can also supply a sample, one outcome drawn according to its probabilities:

\[ y\sim\operatorname{Categorical}(p_1,\ldots,p_K) \tag{6.5}\]

The symbol \(y\) here is a sampled output, rather than a supplied training target. The notation \(\sim\) means “drawn from.” For probabilities \((0.7,0.2,0.1)\), the cumulative intervals are \([0,0.7)\), \([0.7,0.9)\), and \([0.9,1)\). A uniform random draw of 0.75 lies in the second interval and selects class index 1 under zero-based indexing.

The distribution is now suitable for selection or comparison with a target. Before using it, we must ensure its implementation preserves the mathematics when scores are large.

6.3 Stable normalization

For logits \([1000,1001]\), the relative difference is only 1. The probabilities should be ordinary finite numbers. Directly computing \(e^{1000}\) on a computer can nevertheless overflow before division has a chance to cancel the large scale.

Floating-point arithmetic represents numbers with finite precision and a finite range. Overflow occurs when a result exceeds that range. Numerical stability concerns whether an implementation obtains useful results despite those finite representations.

Softmax’s invariance to a common shift provides the remedy. Let \(m\) be the largest logit in a row. Subtract it before exponentiation:

\[ \operatorname{softmax}(z)_i=\frac{\exp(z_i-\max(z))}{\sum_j\exp(z_j-\max(z))} \tag{6.6}\]

Every numerator is multiplied by \(e^{-m}\), and so is the denominator. The factor cancels, leaving the same distribution. The largest shifted score is zero, whose exponential is 1. All other shifted scores are nonpositive, so their exponentials are at most 1.

For \([1000,1001]\), subtracting 1001 gives \([-1,0]\). Exponentiation gives approximately \([0.3679,1]\). Dividing by 1.3679 yields \([0.2689,0.7311]\). The large common scale has disappeared without changing the intended probabilities.

Sometimes we need log probabilities directly. The log-sum-exp operation calculates the logarithm of the sum of exponentials:

\[ \operatorname{logsumexp}(z)=m+\log\left(\sum_j\exp(z_j-m)\right),\quad m=\max_j z_j \tag{6.7}\]

To obtain the stable form, factor \(e^m\) out of the sum. The logarithm of that factor contributes \(m\), and the remaining sum contains the shifted exponentials. For \([1,2]\), the result is \(2+\log(e^{-1}+1)\approx2.313\). For \([1000,1001]\), it is approximately 1001.313.

The log probability of candidate \(i\) is \(z_i-\operatorname{logsumexp}(z)\). Libraries can calculate this directly with log_softmax, avoiding an intermediate probability that might round to zero before a later logarithm. The loss chapter uses these log probabilities to score observed targets.

The stable formula assumes a nonempty row of finite logits. Extremely small probabilities can still underflow to zero, and nonfinite input values need a separate policy. In particular, excluding every candidate does not leave a distribution to normalize. The later masking examples will state what should happen in that case.

Scores can now support two different uses. With a fixed model, a selection rule produces an output. During training, the probability assigned to an observed target supplies a loss. We first follow the output-selection branch.

Three independent binary examples have logits \([0.9,-0.2,0.6]\). Why is applying softmax across these three values a different model from applying sigmoid to each? What happens to a categorical distribution when 1000 is added to every logit?

Feedback: Softmax would make the examples compete for one total probability mass. Sigmoid gives each example its own positive-outcome probability and complement. Adding a common constant to categorical logits leaves their softmax probabilities unchanged, although a direct exponential implementation may overflow. Subtracting the row maximum avoids that intermediate overflow.
