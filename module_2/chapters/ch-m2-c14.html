14  Word2Vec

An embedding lookup returns coordinates, but arbitrary coordinates need not support useful comparisons. Text supplies a training signal: nearby words reveal repeated patterns of usage. Word2Vec turns those patterns into prediction tasks that adjust the embedding rows.

We first construct the training examples, then inspect what the resulting vectors represent. Their strengths and limitations explain why later models also need to process word order and context.

14.1 Learning from context

The lookup in Chapter 13 establishes how tokens select parameters. To train those parameters without human labels, we can create targets from neighboring words. The distributional hypothesis is that words with similar uses tend to occur in similar surroundings. It supplies a learning signal without guaranteeing that nearby vectors have identical meanings.

A context window selects positions around one center word. For “The cat sat on the mat”, choose “sat” at zero-based position 2 and a window radius of two. Its context is “The”, “cat”, “on”, and “the”. The final “mat” lies outside this window.

Skip-gram predicts surrounding words from the center word. This window creates four positive training pairs: \((\text{sat},\text{The})\), \((\text{sat},\text{cat})\), \((\text{sat},\text{on})\), and \((\text{sat},\text{the})\). Case remains distinct in this example. Lowercasing the corpus would merge “The” and “the” before pair construction.

Across the corpus, skip-gram maximizes the log probabilities of these observed contexts.

\[ \operatorname*{maximize}\;\sum_{t}\sum_{c\in\operatorname{context}(t)}\log P(x_{c}\mid x_{t}) \tag{14.1}\]

Here \(x_t\) is a center token and \(x_c\) is a token at a selected context position. At sentence boundaries, fewer neighbors are available unless preprocessing supplies boundary tokens.

Continuous bag-of-words, or CBOW, reverses the prediction direction. It combines the surrounding vectors, commonly by averaging, and predicts the center token from that representation.

\[ \operatorname*{maximize}\;\sum_{t}\log P(x_{t}\mid\operatorname{context}(t)) \tag{14.2}\]

Skip-gram creates a prediction for each center-context pair. CBOW combines a window into one prediction. Neither objective requires manually assigned topic labels, and neither preserves the exact order inside a bag of context words.

Word2Vec uses separate center vectors \(v_c\) and context vectors \(u_o\). Each has \(d\) coordinates. Their dot product \(u_o^\top v_c\) scores a pair. A full softmax could normalize these scores over the vocabulary, using the mechanism in Chapter 6.

For a large vocabulary, calculating every context score at every step is expensive. Negative sampling trains a smaller binary discrimination task: distinguish observed pairs from pairs produced by a noise sampler. A sampled pair receives target zero for that update.

For one observed pair and \(K\) sampled words, the expected objective to maximize is:

\[ \log\sigma(u_{o}^{T}v_{c})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{n_{k}}\!\left[\log\sigma(-u_{n_{k}}^{T}v_{c})\right] \tag{14.3}\]

The first term rewards a large observed score. Each expectation averages over a draw \(n_k\) from a chosen noise distribution. In practice, a step uses the actual sampled words and minimizes the negative of their summed terms.

\[ -\log\sigma(u_{o}^{T}v_{c})-\sum_{k}\log\sigma(-u_{k}^{T}v_{c}) \tag{14.4}\]

Here \(u_k\) is the context vector of the \(k\)th sampled word. The sampler usually favors frequent vocabulary items, with its distribution chosen during training. This objective avoids a full vocabulary normalization. Its sigmoid score estimates the discrimination task, not a normalized probability over possible next words.

For a center vector \((1,0)\) and observed context \((2,0)\), the score is 2 and \(\sigma(2)\approx0.881\). A sampled context \((-1,0)\) has score \(-1\), giving \(\sigma(-1)\approx0.269\).

The observed term contributes \(-\log\sigma(2)\approx0.126928\). The sampled term contributes \(-\log\sigma(1)\approx0.313262\). Their total loss is approximately \(0.440190\).

The score derivatives from Section 10.2 explain the updates. The positive score has derivative \(\sigma(2)-1\approx-0.1192\), while the sampled score has derivative \(\sigma(-1)\approx0.2689\). Gradient descent therefore raises the first score and lowers the second, considered as independent scores.

Return to the pair \((\text{sat},\text{cat})\) with three-coordinate vectors \(v_{\text{sat}}=(0.2,-0.5,0.8)\) and \(u_{\text{cat}}=(0.1,0.4,-0.3)\). Their score is \(0.02-0.20-0.24=-0.42\), giving \(\sigma(-0.42)\approx0.3965\). The positive-pair objective favors a larger dot product.

Suppose the noise draws are “mat” and “on”. The latter also occurs in the true context window. A noise sample is therefore not a claim that two words never occur together. A sampler can draw an observed neighbor unless the implementation explicitly rejects it.

Updates change shared coordinates, so improving one pair can affect many others. A larger dot product also need not mean a smaller Euclidean distance. Both direction and vector length contribute to the score.

With \(B\) positive pairs and \(K\) samples per pair, center and positive-context lookups have shape \([B,d]\). Sampled IDs have shape \([B,K]\), and their vectors have shape \([B,K,d]\). Dot products produce \(B\) positive scores and \(B K\) sampled scores, rather than \(B|V|\) full-vocabulary scores.

The following Gensim example trains on three short token sequences. sg=1 selects skip-gram, window=2 sets the maximum context radius, and vector_size=8 sets the row width. min_count=1 retains every observed word.

Code example: Training and inspecting a small Word2Vec model

from gensim.models import Word2Vec

sentences = [
    ["cat", "sat", "mat"],
    ["dog", "sat", "rug"],
    ["cat", "chased", "dog"],
]

model = Word2Vec(
    sentences,
    vector_size=8,
    window=2,
    min_count=1,
    sg=1,
    epochs=100,
    workers=1,
    seed=7,
)
print(model.wv["cat"].shape)
print(model.wv.similarity("cat", "dog"))
print(model.wv.most_similar("cat", topn=2))

The vector shape is \((8,)\). The printed similarity and neighbors illustrate inspection operations, but three sentences cannot establish reliable language relationships. A fixed seed and one worker reduce run variability without making this corpus adequate.

The course notebook instead trains on a CSV of research-paper abstracts. Its reusable iterator lowercases tokens with spaCy, and Gensim rereads it across training passes. The corresponding example expects a local, headerless file with an identifier and abstract in each row.

Code example: Training on a reusable local abstract corpus

import csv
import spacy
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

class Corpus:
    def __init__(self, filename):
        self.filename = filename
        self.nlp = spacy.blank("en")

    def __iter__(self):
        with open(self.filename, encoding="utf-8", newline="") as source:
            for identifier, abstract in csv.reader(source):
                yield [token.text.lower() for token in self.nlp(abstract)]

documents = Corpus("arxiv.csv")
abstract_model = Word2Vec(
    documents, min_count=100, window=5, vector_size=100,
    sg=0, workers=1, seed=7,
)

This configuration uses CBOW, width 100, maximum window radius 5, and minimum frequency 100. Removing rare words reduces storage but also removes the ability to inspect their vectors. Increasing width creates more parameters and may require more data to estimate them usefully.

The objective determines what the table learns. Inspection now asks whether that result supports the intended comparison.

14.2 Comparing word vectors

Two words can receive related vectors because they occur in similar contexts. A numerical comparison makes that relationship inspectable, but does not turn the vectors into dictionary definitions.

Cosine similarity compares the directions of two nonzero vectors. It divides their dot product by the product of their Euclidean lengths.

\[ \cos(u,v)=\frac{u^{T}v}{\lVert u\rVert_{2}\,\lVert v\rVert_{2}} \tag{14.5}\]

For \(u=(1,0)\) and \(v=(1,1)\), the dot product is 1 and the lengths are 1 and \(\sqrt2\). Their cosine is \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.7071\). Multiplying either vector by a positive constant leaves the cosine unchanged, although it changes the dot product.

A nearest neighbor is the candidate with the largest selected similarity score. Normalizing candidate rows and multiplying them by a normalized query calculates their cosine scores together. Zero vectors need separate handling because their cosine is undefined.

Gensim exposes the learned vectors through model.wv. similarity compares two words, while most_similar ranks vocabulary candidates. similar_by_word performs a single-word neighbor query.

An analogy query combines vectors before searching. The familiar expression “king minus man plus woman” may return “queen” in some trained spaces. Its success depends on the corpus, vocabulary, and objective. Vector arithmetic does not guarantee a linguistic rule.

The notebook applies the same operation to vocabulary tokens for model families. It compares a transformer with BERT, then asks for a corresponding relation to an LSTM. These strings identify architectures explained in Chapter 15 and Chapter 17. The inspection operation only needs their stored vectors.

Sense steering adds or subtracts a context word from a query. Comparing neighbors of “tree” with neighbors of “tree minus syntax” can reveal different usage patterns. This changes the query vector. It does not create a new context-dependent table row for “tree”.

An odd-one-out query returns the item least compatible with the others under the library’s vector comparison. Its result is evidence about that embedding space, rather than a guaranteed taxonomy.

The following queries reproduce these inspection types for the research-abstract model. The short forms nmt, smt, and ner denote neural translation, statistical translation, and entity recognition in the notebook’s corpus. Every requested word must be present before the query can run.

Code example: Inspecting vectors, neighbors, analogies and query direction

wv = abstract_model.wv
groups = [
    "lstm cnn gru svm transformer".split(),
    "bert word2vec gpt-2 roberta xlnet".split(),
    "word2vec bert glove fasttext elmo".split(),
]
required = {"nlp", "nmt", "smt", "ner", "bert", "transformer", "lstm", "tree", "syntax"}
required.update(word for group in groups for word in group)
missing = sorted(required.difference(wv.key_to_index))
if missing:
    raise ValueError(f"Inspection words absent from this vocabulary: {missing}")

print(wv["nlp"].shape)
print(wv.similarity("nmt", "smt"), wv.similarity("nmt", "ner"))
print(wv.similar_by_word("bert", topn=10))
print(wv.most_similar(positive=["transformer", "lstm"], negative=["bert"], topn=1))
print(wv.most_similar(positive=["tree"], topn=10))
print(wv.most_similar(positive=["tree"], negative=["syntax"], topn=10))
for group in groups:
    print(group, wv.doesnt_match(group))

Record returned candidates and scores, then inspect representative corpus contexts. Antonyms can be close because they occur in similar sentences. A technical corpus may also give a common word a specialized neighborhood. An unexpected result calls for checking the data and query before changing the distance measure.

High-dimensional neighborhoods are difficult to inspect visually. Dimension reduction maps their vectors to fewer coordinates. The notebook uses t-SNE, a method that arranges points to reflect local similarity relationships in a two-dimensional plot.

The plot begins with one complete vector per selected word. The following code stacks them into a matrix with shape \([N,d]\), including the target exactly once. It checks for zero vectors before using cosine distance.

Code example: Plotting one complete vector per selected word

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE

wv = abstract_model.wv
target_word = "bert"
selected_words = [target_word] + [
    word for word, score in wv.most_similar(target_word, topn=200)
    if word != target_word
]
selected_words = list(dict.fromkeys(selected_words))
embeddings = np.stack([wv[word] for word in selected_words])
if len(selected_words) < 3 or np.any(np.linalg.norm(embeddings, axis=1) == 0):
    raise ValueError("Use at least three nonzero vectors for this plot")
mapped = TSNE(
    n_components=2, metric="cosine", init="random", random_state=7,
    perplexity=min(30, len(selected_words) - 1),
).fit_transform(embeddings)
assert mapped.shape == (len(selected_words), 2)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 12))
ax.scatter(mapped[:, 0], mapped[:, 1])
for word, (x, y) in zip(selected_words, mapped):
    ax.annotate(word, (x, y), fontsize=7)
ax.set_title("A two-dimensional view of the selected word vectors")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("word-vector-neighborhood.png", dpi=160)
plt.close(fig)

The result has one plotted point and label per input word. The perplexity parameter controls a local-neighborhood scale and must be smaller than the number of points. Here it belongs to t-SNE, not the language-model evaluation measure in Chapter 8.

The code uses a random initial layout with a fixed seed. The source notebook uses principal-component initialization, which starts from major directions of variation. Either choice requires checking that conclusions remain reasonable under different plotting settings.

Nearby points can help locate candidates for inspection. Distances between separate clusters, cluster sizes, and empty regions need not preserve the original geometry. Check any important relationship with cosine scores in the original space.

Separately trained languages can use different coordinate systems even when their word relationships are similar. Cross-language alignment learns a mapping between those systems from paired translations. The linear mapping method uses monolingual word vectors with a smaller bilingual dictionary.

With row-vector matrices \(X\) and \(Y\) containing matched words, fit \(Q\) to reduce \(\lVert XQ-Y\rVert_F^2\). The squared Frobenius norm adds squared errors across every matrix entry. Transform a new source vector with \(Q\), then compare it with target-language vectors using a consistent similarity rule.

For a toy alignment, source vectors \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) correspond to target vectors \((0,1)\) and \((-1,0)\). Matrix \(Q=[[0,1],[-1,0]]\) maps both pairs exactly. It maps \((1,1)\) to \((-1,1)\). This arithmetic illustrates a common coordinate transformation, not a translation-quality result.

The method requires useful paired anchors and compatible geometric relationships. Ambiguous meanings and domain differences can break a simple linear fit. Evaluate held-out translations before relying on its nearest neighbors.

14.3 Compressing co-occurrence

Predictive training is one way to obtain compact vectors. Another starts with a matrix of observed counts and asks which directions retain most of its structure. This view connects representation learning to matrix compression.

A co-occurrence matrix records how often words appear with selected context features. Its rows may be large and sparse. A matrix’s rank counts its independent directions, bounded by its smaller dimension.

Singular value decomposition, or SVD, separates a matrix into orthogonal left directions, nonnegative singular values, and orthogonal right directions. Keeping the \(r\) largest singular values gives a rank-\(r\) approximation:

\[ M\approx U_{r}\Sigma_{r}V_{r}^{T} \tag{14.6}\]

For \(M\) with shape \([m,n]\), \(U_r\) has shape \([m,r]\) and \(V_r^\top\) has shape \([r,n]\). The diagonal matrix \(\Sigma_r\) stores \(r\) retained singular values. Multiplying these factors reconstructs the retained component of \(M\).

Consider \(M=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\). Keeping only its first direction gives \(U_1=(1,0)^\top\), singular value 3, and \(V_1^\top=(1,0)\). Their product is \(\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\). The discarded entry contributes squared reconstruction error 1.

For a \(1{,}000\times500\) matrix, dense storage holds 500,000 values. With rank 50, the left factor holds 50,000 values, the right holds 25,000, and the diagonal holds 50. The total is 75,050 values. This saves space when \(r\) is much smaller than both original dimensions, but some structure may be discarded.

An autoencoder learns a related compression through reconstruction. Its encoder maps input \(x\) to a narrower vector \(h\), and its decoder maps \(h\) to a reconstruction \(\hat x\). A loss such as \(\|x-\hat x\|^2\) rewards retaining information needed to rebuild the input.

For a two-coordinate input \(x=(2,4)\), let the encoder produce \(h=(x_1+x_2)/2=3\). A decoder that repeats \(h\) returns \(\hat x=(3,3)\), giving squared error \(1+1=2\). Input \((3,3)\) has the same code and zero reconstruction error. This one-coordinate bottleneck cannot distinguish the two inputs under these mappings.

Training changes the encoder and decoder to reduce the selected reconstruction loss across examples. For text, a decoder can reconstruct token targets using cross-entropy, while the internal vector provides a sequence representation. Good reconstruction alone does not guarantee that this vector organizes sentences by the similarity a retrieval task needs.

Affine and nonlinear autoencoders have different expressive limits, following Chapter 12. Here “decoder” means the reconstruction component. Its presence does not imply the next-token generation architecture introduced later.

Compressed count factors and predictive embeddings can both provide fixed vectors. Their objectives preserve different properties, and a small representation is not automatically suitable for every downstream task.

14.4 Static and contextual representations

Useful word neighborhoods still leave an important ambiguity. The same word can have different roles in different sentences. One stored row cannot change with its current surroundings.

A static embedding assigns the same vector to every occurrence of a token type. A contextual representation also depends on the sequence being processed. A hidden state is an internal vector produced during that processing.

The word “bank” selects one static row in both “river bank” and “bank account”. A sequence model can combine that row with its neighbors to produce different hidden states. The initial lookup can remain shared while the later representation changes.

Static vectors remain useful for compact baselines and inexpensive word comparisons. Tasks that depend on word order or a particular occurrence require additional processing. Chapter 15 begins with a limited history and then develops a state that changes as a sequence is read.
