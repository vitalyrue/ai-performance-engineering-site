9  Validation and testing

A small training loss shows that a model fits the examples used to adjust it. It does not establish that the same rule will help on new inputs. Evaluation needs separate data, a task-appropriate measurement, and an account of which errors matter.

This chapter turns those requirements into a comparison procedure. It distinguishes errors in class decisions, the ordering of model scores, and the reliability of reported probabilities.

9.1 Generalization

Suppose an article classifier performs well on the articles used during training but fails on new reports. Its fitted parameters may capture recurring topic evidence, details peculiar to those articles, or both. Generalization is useful prediction on new examples from the intended task distribution.

The data-split roles separate fitting from this evaluation. Training examples adjust the model. Validation examples guide model choices. Test examples estimate performance after those choices are fixed. A hyperparameter is a setting chosen outside the parameter-fitting calculation, such as model width or learning rate.

Held-out loss uses the same loss and reduction as training, applied to different examples with fixed parameters. Two held-out losses of 0.2 and 0.5 have mean 0.35. Their arithmetic matches the empirical-risk average. Their experimental role differs because held-out losses do not supply a parameter update.

Data leakage occurs when information meant to be held out influences fitting or selection. It can enter through vocabulary fitting, feature selection, duplicated documents, or repeated use of test scores. The fixed-vocabulary example in Chapter 4 prevents one such route.

The split must match the kind of new input expected. Near-duplicate articles should not appear on both sides of a supposedly independent comparison. Records from the same person or event may require grouping. If the application predicts future outcomes, a random split can expose later information during fitting, so a time-based split may be appropriate. Benchmark material appearing in pretraining data creates a related concern for language-model evaluation.

Learning curves compare training and validation measurements as training progresses or data increases. Low training loss with much higher validation loss suggests that the fitted rule transfers poorly. Overfitting describes fitting details of the training sample that do not support the intended new-data behavior. A model that cannot fit important patterns even in training may be underfitting.

The terms bias and variance help describe possible causes. A restrictive representation can impose systematic errors across samples, contributing to bias. A fitted model that changes substantially with the training sample can contribute high variance. A gap in two curves alone does not prove either cause, and high training loss may also indicate a training failure.

These distinctions guide experiments. A poor fit on both splits can motivate checking features, model capacity, and the training procedure. A good training fit with a large validation gap can motivate more representative data, regularization, or a simpler model. Regularization constrains fitting to discourage solutions that depend too strongly on sample-specific details. Early stopping chooses a training checkpoint using validation behavior. Both must be selected without repeatedly consulting the final test set.

Training loss need not fall at every update. The important evaluation question is whether a chosen procedure improves the held-out measurements that matter, under a fixed comparison protocol.

9.2 Classification errors

An overall correctness rate can hide the kind of mistakes an application cannot tolerate. For binary classification, choose which outcome counts as positive, then record both the actual and predicted class. A confusion matrix retains the resulting four counts.

A true positive is an actual positive predicted positive. A false negative is an actual positive predicted negative. A false positive is an actual negative predicted positive, and a true negative is an actual negative predicted negative. Their abbreviations are TP, FN, FP, and TN.

The figure arranges these outcomes by actual and predicted class. Check the axis labels before reading a library’s matrix, because a transpose exchanges the roles of rows and columns.

Use a test set with TP=30, FN=10, FP=10, and TN=50. It contains 40 actual positives, 60 actual negatives, and 100 examples in total. Several metrics answer different questions about those same outcomes.

Accuracy measures the fraction of all examples predicted correctly:

\[ \mathrm{accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN} \tag{9.1}\]

Here it is \((30+50)/100=0.80\). Accuracy can conceal imbalance. If almost every example is negative, always predicting negative can score highly while finding no positive cases.

Precision asks how many positive predictions are correct:

\[ \mathrm{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \tag{9.2}\]

Its denominator is the number predicted positive. The example gives \(30/(30+10)=0.75\). Recall, also called sensitivity, asks how many actual positives were found:

\[ \mathrm{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \tag{9.3}\]

Its denominator is the number actually positive. Here recall is also \(30/(30+10)=0.75\), though these metrics need not be equal. F1 combines precision \(P\) and recall \(R\) through their harmonic mean:

\[ F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R} \tag{9.4}\]

For \(P=R=0.75\), F1 is 0.75. The harmonic mean penalizes a large imbalance between precision and recall. It still omits true negatives, so it cannot replace every application-specific measurement.

Specificity asks how many actual negatives were correctly rejected: \(\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})\). The example gives \(50/60\approx0.833\). Negative predictive value, or NPV, asks how many negative predictions are correct: \(\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})\). It also gives \(50/60\approx0.833\) here, with a different denominator meaning.

These ratios need valid denominators. Precision is undefined when no example is predicted positive, and recall is undefined when the evaluated set has no actual positives. Report such cases and the library’s handling policy. Silently substituting a favorable value can conceal the missing evidence.

For multiclass prediction, inspect the full matrix before reducing it to one score. Per-class precision and recall can treat each class as positive against the rest. A macro average gives each class equal weight. A micro average combines the relevant counts before computing the metric. Multilabel tasks additionally allow several labels per example, so their decision thresholds and averaging convention must be stated.

The preferred balance depends on the consequences of false positives and false negatives. A threshold makes that balance an explicit decision.

9.3 Thresholds, ranking, and confidence

A decision threshold converts a score or probability into an action. Under the rule “predict positive when \(p\geq\tau\),” lowering \(\tau\) can only add positive predictions on a fixed dataset. It cannot reduce either the number of true positives or false positives. Raising it can only remove such predictions. Precision, as a ratio of two changing counts, need not move monotonically.

If false positives and false negatives have different costs, compare them directly on validation data. For calibrated probabilities and fixed error costs, a positive decision has expected error cost \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}(1-p)\). A negative decision has expected error cost \(C_{\mathrm{FN}}p\). Choosing the smaller gives threshold \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}/(C_{\mathrm{FP}}+C_{\mathrm{FN}})\), assuming correct decisions have zero cost. Different costs or unreliable probabilities require a different analysis.

A receiver operating characteristic, or ROC, curve records the outcomes of sweeping the threshold. Its vertical coordinate is the true-positive rate, which equals recall. Its horizontal coordinate is the false-positive rate:

\[ \mathrm{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN} \tag{9.5}\]

Both actual classes must be present for these rates to be defined. With TP=40, FN=10, FP=20, and TN=30, the coordinates are TPR=\(40/50=0.8\) and FPR=\(20/50=0.4\). Each threshold supplies another point.

The area under the ROC curve, or ROC AUC, summarizes the resulting ranking behavior:

\[ \mathrm{AUC}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{TPR}(\mathrm{FPR})\,d\mathrm{FPR} \tag{9.6}\]

The integral accumulates area as the false-positive rate moves from 0 to 1. For the piecewise-linear curve through \((0,0)\), \((0.5,0.8)\), and \((1,1)\), the two trapezoids have areas \(0.5(0+0.8)/2=0.2\) and \(0.5(0.8+1)/2=0.45\). Their total is 0.65.

Calibration concerns a different property: whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. Among many comparable predictions near 0.8, a calibrated model should be correct about 80 percent of the time. Grouping predictions into probability ranges and comparing confidence with observed outcomes gives a reliability check. Small groups provide uncertain estimates, so counts belong beside those comparisons.

A strictly increasing transformation of scores preserves their ordering and ROC curve, while it can change their probability interpretation. Good ranking therefore does not establish good calibration or select the right operating threshold.

The runnable scikit-learn example uses six fixed labels and scores. It applies threshold 0.5 for the confusion-matrix metrics and retains the original scores for ROC AUC.

Code example: Confusion-matrix metrics and ROC AUC from fixed predictions

from sklearn.metrics import (
    confusion_matrix,
    precision_recall_fscore_support,
    roc_auc_score,
)

y_true = [1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0]
scores = [0.9, 0.4, 0.8, 0.2, 0.7, 0.1]
y_pred = [int(score >= 0.5) for score in scores]

print(confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred))
print(precision_recall_fscore_support(y_true, y_pred, average="binary"))
print(roc_auc_score(y_true, scores))

The matrix is [[2,1],[1,2]] when rows are actual classes and columns are predicted classes in order 0,1. Precision, recall, and F1 are each \(2/3\). ROC AUC is \(7/9\approx0.778\), reflecting seven correctly ordered positive-negative pairs out of nine.

Raising this example’s threshold from 0.5 to 0.8 retains the positive example scored 0.9 and the negative example scored 0.8. Precision falls from \(2/3\) to \(1/2\), demonstrating why a higher threshold does not guarantee higher precision. The six observations make the arithmetic inspectable, but do not support a stable estimate of application performance.

9.4 Comparing experiments

Model scores can mislead when the experiments use different data splits or preprocessing. A fair comparison fixes the data split, preprocessing, target rule, metric, and evaluation procedure while varying a stated setting. It records enough detail to explain why one result differs from another.

Grid search evaluates every combination in a specified set of hyperparameter values. Random search samples combinations from stated ranges or distributions under a fixed budget. Bayesian optimization uses previous evaluations to model promising settings and choose further trials. These methods differ in how trials are chosen. None removes the need for an untouched test set.

For example, comparing five learning rates with three batch sizes requires 15 grid trials. Keep the dataset and evaluation rule fixed, record the training budget for each trial, and compare learning curves as well as the final metric. If training randomness affects the conclusion, repeat representative settings with several seeds and report their variation. The classifier and optimizer experiments provide the course’s concrete protocols.

A benchmark packages a task with data and a scoring procedure. Results are comparable only when their conditions match, including model version, prompt format, allowed tools, decoding policy, and answer extraction. Each score measures performance under its particular protocol. It cannot establish overall model quality by itself.

Massive Multitask Language Understanding, or MMLU, evaluates answers across many academic and professional subjects. Its paper describes the tasks and scoring setup. TruthfulQA tests whether answers reproduce common human falsehoods. HellaSwag tests plausible continuations of described situations. These distinct targets help explain why the three scores cannot substitute for one another.

A language model may answer a classification prompt by generating a label string. Its output is still generated token by token, but the application may score one final class decision. Evaluation must therefore check both whether the output is in the required format and whether its content is correct. Architecture and objective choices connect those task interfaces to the model computation.

Select the model and threshold using validation evidence, then report the final test result without further tuning to it. Retain examples of each important error type. An aggregate score locates a problem only when it can be connected back to the inputs and decisions that produced it.

Chapter checkpoint

A model has strong ROC AUC but its 0.9-confidence predictions are correct only 60 percent of the time. Which property is failing? Why should choosing a threshold from the final test set change how its result is reported?

Feedback: Its probability calibration is poor even if its ranking is useful. Choosing the threshold from test results uses that set for selection, so it no longer provides an untouched final evaluation of that choice. Threshold selection belongs with validation evidence.
