19  Pretraining data and scaling

The architecture and objective define what a model can learn from an example. The corpus determines which examples it receives, while the compute budget limits how many updates can be made. Pretraining quality depends on those decisions together.

The progression from fixed histories to recurrent states and attention changes how context enters a prediction. Large transformer training also combines many parameters with large corpora and sustained optimization. The forward, loss, backward, and update cycle remains the one in Section 10.5.

19.1 Curating the corpus

Training on more text does not guarantee more useful evidence. Repeated documents, broken extraction, and an unsuitable domain mixture can change the errors the model learns to reduce. Corpus curation selects and prepares the material that contributes to the objective.

Filtering removes or downweights records according to explicit criteria, such as language, extraction quality, or intended content scope. Privacy and licensing requirements also constrain which records belong in the corpus. A filter changes the training distribution, so its effects should be inspected across sources and languages.

Deduplication detects exact or near-duplicate examples. Exact matching catches identical records, while near-duplicate methods can find repeated passages with small changes. Overly broad matching can remove distinct examples, so thresholds need sample review.

Repeating one record gives its patterns more influence on the loss. Removing duplicates therefore changes both the token count and the relative weight of evidence. It may also reduce memorization caused by excessive repetition.

Evaluation contamination occurs when training includes evaluation examples or closely related answers. It can raise a reported score without demonstrating generalization. Keep evaluation sets separate and check suspected overlaps using the split principles in Chapter 9.

Record which filtering and deduplication rules were applied, their removal counts, and representative retained and rejected examples. A changed corpus should be explainable through those decisions, rather than only its final token count.

After selecting records, training still needs a rule for how often to sample each remaining source.

19.2 Mixing sources

A corpus can contain code, books, articles, and other sources in very different quantities. Source-mixture weights specify how often a sampler selects each source. They need not match the sources’ raw sizes.

Let \(P_s\) describe source \(s\), and let \(\alpha_s\ge0\) be its sampling probability. The resulting mixture is:

\[ P_{\mathrm{data}}=\sum_s\alpha_sP_s,\quad\sum_s\alpha_s=1 \tag{19.1}\]

The sampler first chooses a source with probability \(\alpha_s\), then draws an example from it. With code weight \(0.3\) and book weight \(0.7\), ten draws contain three code examples and seven book examples in expectation. An actual group of ten can differ.

The sampling unit matters. If code examples average two tokens and book examples average six, those expected draws contribute six code tokens and forty-two book tokens. Code then supplies \(6/48=12.5\%\) of tokens despite accounting for \(30\%\) of examples.

Thus an example mixture is not automatically a token mixture. For a mean loss over valid tokens, longer examples contribute more target positions. A mean of per-example means would weight examples differently, following the reduction discussion in Section 8.2.

Oversampling selects a source more often than its raw corpus share would imply. This can strengthen a scarce domain, but may repeat the same examples many times. Track both processed tokens and unique material to distinguish added training exposure from added evidence.

The chosen objective then creates labels from those records. Causal, masked, and source-target formats follow Chapter 18. Changing the source weights alters the empirical distribution of their losses without changing the underlying gradient mechanism.

Sampling decides which examples enter a batch. Packing decides how their token positions share the allocated tensor space.

19.3 Packing sequences

Padding every short document to the model’s full context length can waste allocated positions. Sequence packing places several shorter examples into a longer training array. Its boundary rules determine whether those examples remain independent.

A separator token makes a boundary visible in the text, but does not by itself block attention across it. If independent documents are intended, an attention mask must stop queries in one document from reading another. A corresponding loss mask must exclude unintended boundary transitions.

Consider document A with tokens [BOS, 4, 8, EOS] and document B with [BOS, 6, EOS]. Packing them into eight positions gives [BOS, 4, 8, EOS, BOS, 6, EOS, PAD].

The first seven input positions have following targets [4, 8, EOS, BOS, 6, EOS, PAD]. For independent-document training, valid target indicators are [1, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0]. Predicting B’s BOS from A’s EOS is excluded, as is predicting padding.

Within each document, attention remains causal. Queries at B’s positions can read B’s prefix but cannot read A. This combines the visibility and validity rules from Section 16.3 with the target reduction from Section 8.2.

A different corpus policy may deliberately train one continuous stream across separators. That is a different conditioning assumption and should be recorded. A packed tensor’s shape does not reveal which policy was used.

Supervised-token utilization measures the share of allocated positions that contribute targets to the objective.

\[ \operatorname{utilization}=\frac{\mathrm{supervised\,tokens}}{\mathrm{allocated\,positions}} \tag{19.2}\]

If 900 of 1,024 allocated positions are supervised, utilization is \(900/1024=0.878906\), or about \(87.9\%\). The small packed example above has five supervised targets among eight allocated positions.

This measure differs from hardware utilization. Boundary markers, masked prompts, and padding can occupy real tensor positions without contributing loss. Kernel efficiency, sequence lengths, and device scheduling also affect elapsed time.

Packing can reduce wasted space, but it cannot repair an unsuitable corpus or objective. Once the data procedure is defined, the remaining planning question is how to divide compute between model size and training exposure.

19.4 Allocating compute

A larger model costs more to process each token. Under a fixed training budget, increasing parameter count usually leaves fewer tokens to train on. Compute-optimal training chooses their combination to minimize the selected loss within that budget.

A scaling law is an empirical relationship fitted to measured runs. One useful form separates a limiting loss from parameter- and data-related corrections.

\[ L(N,D)\approx L_{\infty}+aN^{-\alpha}+bD^{-\beta} \tag{19.3}\]

Here \(N\) is parameter count, \(D\) is processed training-token count, and \(a,b,\alpha,\beta\) are positive fitted constants. The symbol \(D\) denotes tokens in this section, rather than the hidden width used in earlier chapters.

A simple dense-transformer planning estimate is \(C\approx6ND\) floating-point operations. It captures major parameter-dependent forward and backward work. Attention length, architecture, and implementation can require a more detailed estimate.

For \(N=10^9\) parameters and \(D=20\times10^9\) tokens, this estimate gives \(C=1.2\times10^{20}\) operations. Doubling parameters and halving tokens leaves the product, and thus this estimated compute, unchanged. It does not leave the fitted loss unchanged.

Substituting \(D=C/(6N)\) into the loss estimate gives \(L(N)\approx L_\infty+aN^{-\alpha}+b(6N/C)^\beta\). Increasing \(N\) reduces its parameter correction but increases its data correction. Differentiating with respect to \(\log N\) balances these effects at \(\alpha aN^{-\alpha}=\beta b(6N/C)^\beta\).

For this simplified fit, the balance implies \(N_{\text{opt}}\propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}\) and \(D_{\text{opt}}\propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}\). When the exponents are similar, both allocations grow approximately with the square root of compute.

The Chinchilla study found approximately balanced growth of parameters and tokens within its studied range. Its 70-billion-parameter model used 1.4 trillion training tokens. The resulting twenty-to-one ratio motivates a familiar planning heuristic:

\[ D_{\mathrm{tokens}}\approx20N_{\mathrm{params}} \tag{19.4}\]

Under that heuristic, a seven-billion-parameter model suggests roughly 140 billion training tokens. This is an estimate from a particular training-cost objective, rather than a universal target for every corpus and architecture.

A separate arithmetic example illustrates how estimated loss components combine. A limiting term of 1.0, parameter correction of 0.2, and data correction of 0.1 give 1.3. If a separately estimated optimization gap adds 0.05, the result is 1.35. These hypothetical values are not a fitted forecast.

Training compute, parameter count, and token count are coupled. They cannot all be varied independently while retaining a fixed compute budget. Fitted trends also depend on data quality, training recipes, and the range of observed runs.

Deployment adds another objective. A smaller model trained longer may be useful when its lower serving cost matters across many requests. Evaluate total training and serving requirements alongside quality, rather than selecting a model from parameter count alone.

Once a base model exists, adapting it can be more practical than repeating pretraining. Chapter 20 compares changes to context, behavior, and trainable parameters.

Chapter checkpoint

A sampler draws 30% of its documents from code. Why might code contribute much less than 30% of the token loss?

Feedback: Its documents may be shorter or contain fewer supervised positions. Inspect the sampling unit, valid target counts, and loss reduction before changing the weights.

Packed documents contain separator tokens. What additional evidence establishes that they are independent training examples?

Feedback: Inspect attention across document boundaries and the labels at each boundary. Separators alone do not block conditioning, and a full tensor can still contain invalid cross-document targets.
